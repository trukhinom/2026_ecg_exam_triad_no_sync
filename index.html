Physiological signal analysis

Triad test

Baseline / Exam

1. Research question

Can interpersonal cardiac synchrony be detected within a small group (triad) during a shared exam situation, and does the degree of synchrony differ depending on participants' role in that situation — peers taking the exam together versus a lector?

Limitations

This is a single triad, a single session — the result is best read as a pilot observation, not a confirmed group-level finding about students and lecturers.

3.3 DTW distance matrix

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW): unlike Pearson correlation, which compares two series strictly point-by-point at the same moment in time, DTW finds the best nonlinear alignment between them — letting one series stretch or compress relative to the other — and returns the cost of that alignment. Lower cost means the SHAPE of the two RMSSD curves matches more closely, even if events in them are offset in time; unlike WCC, it doesn't ask whether the two moved together at the same instant.

Fits a summary synchrony coefficient for a participant pair — DTW distance. The diagonal is self-similarity: 0.0 is natural for DTW distance (a series' distance to itself). Lower = more similar RMSSD curve shape. In Exam, the insider pair has the lowest (most similar) DTW of the three — consistent with the hypothesis. In Baseline, that's not the case: one outsider pair (Participant 2 × 3) is actually closer than the insider pair. The matrix is symmetric by construction (synchrony(A,B) = synchrony(B,A)). DTW has no built-in significance test here — read it comparatively (which pair's curve shape is closer), not as a significant/not-significant result.

How to read a cell: it's the cost of "warping" one person's RMSSD curve to line up with another's — NOT a percentage or a correlation, so there's no fixed scale to judge a single number against (0.59 isn't "59% similar" to anything). The only useful comparison is between the three cells in the same matrix.

DTW alignment example

What's happening here: both lines are the same z-scored RMSSD curve as in 3.2 — one drawn above the other (not overlaid) so the connectors below are readable. Each thin gray line connects one point on the top curve to the point on the bottom curve that DTW matched it to.

How to read a connector: a near-vertical line means the two points matched up at roughly the same time — no shift was needed. A slanted or long diagonal line means DTW had to reach forward or back in time to find a matching shape — the longer and more tilted, the bigger that shift. A cluster of many steep diagonal lines in one stretch (for example, look for where the lines fan out most) means that stretch of the two people's RMSSD curves needed the most reshaping to line up. The single number in the 3.2 matrix (e.g. 0.56) is just the total cost of all these connectors added up and averaged — this chart is what that one number is actually built from.

3.4 Synchrony Network

The same DTW distance from 3.2, but rescaled so that HIGHER means MORE similar (weight = 1 / (1 + DTW distance)) — the opposite direction from the matrix, where lower means more similar. The numbers here are therefore different from the matrix (e.g. DTW 0.59 becomes weight 0.63) even though they come from the exact same underlying computation. A thicker, closer edge means a stronger link. With only 3 participants the layout always settles into roughly a triangle regardless of the pattern — this chart doesn't add new information beyond the 3.2 matrix, it's the same comparison, just easier to scan at a glance than comparing decimals.

In Baseline, Participant 2 × 3 (an outsider pair) has the strongest link; the insider pair (1 × 2) is weakest. In Exam, that flips — the insider pair becomes strongest and Participant 2 × 3 becomes weakest. As with 3.2, there's no significance test behind these weights — read this as a visual summary of the same comparative pattern, not a tested result.

4. Discussion

No statistically significant synchrony was found between the triad's participants. An unexpected finding was the rise in RMSSD during the exam relative to the pre-exam phase — the opposite of what the stress hypothesis would predict. One possible explanation is that anticipating the exam was a more stressful experience than the exam itself.

This interpretation should also weigh a duration asymmetry: the Exam phase lasted much longer than Baseline. Averaging RMSSD over the whole exam period could have masked an early nervous-system reaction right at the exam's start.

Two data-quality confounds are worth flagging also. For the lecturer, movement during the exam is a plausible confound: unlike the seated students, the lecturer likely moved around the room, and movement is a common source of R-peak detection artifacts that can inflate RMSSD independently of physiology. Second, a clock offset between recording devices could specifically confound the comparison itself, not just add general noise: participant_1 and participant_2 shared a single phone, so their timestamps share one device clock with no cross-device offset, while participant_3 was recorded on a separate phone, giving both outsider pairs a potential cross-device offset the insider pair does not have. Any difference found between their synchrony could therefore partly reflect this technical asymmetry rather than physiology alone — though the coarse time resolution most synchrony methods here use (60-second RMSSD windows) limits how much a likely sub-few-second offset could distort the result; it would matter more for anything comparing raw, sample-level timing, such as the ECG overlay.
