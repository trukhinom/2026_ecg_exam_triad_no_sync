1. Research question

Can interpersonal cardiac synchrony be detected within a small group (triad) during a shared exam situation, and does the degree of synchrony differ depending on participants' role in that situation — peers taking the exam together versus a lector?

3. Synchrony Detection

Below are the results for Windowed Cross-Correlation (WCC), Dynamic Time Warping (DTW), and the Synchrony Network graph.

3.1 WCC

Windowed Cross-Correlation (WCC): a sliding-window Pearson correlation between each pair of participants' instantaneous HR — 60-second window, 10-second step. Unlike a single correlation coefficient over the whole session, this gives a time series showing WHEN a pair is synchronized, not just how much on average.

3.2 DTW distance matrix

Fits a summary synchrony coefficient for a participant pair — DTW distance. The diagonal is self-similarity: 0.0 is natural for DTW distance (a series' distance to itself). The matrix is symmetric by construction (synchrony(A,B) = synchrony(B,A)).

4. Discussion

No statistically significant synchrony was found between the triad's participants. An unexpected finding was the rise in RMSSD during the exam relative to the pre-exam phase — the opposite of what the stress hypothesis would predict. One possible explanation is that anticipating the exam was a more stressful experience than the exam itself.

This interpretation should also weigh a duration asymmetry: the Exam phase lasted much longer than Baseline. Averaging RMSSD over the whole exam period could have masked an early nervous-system reaction right at the exam's start.

Two data-quality confounds are worth flagging also. For the lecturer, movement during the exam is a plausible confound: unlike the seated students, the lecturer likely moved around the room, and movement is a common source of R-peak detection artifacts that can inflate RMSSD independently of physiology. Second, a clock offset between recording devices could specifically confound the comparison itself, not just add general noise: participant_1 and participant_2 shared a single phone, so their timestamps share one device clock with no cross-device offset, while participant_3 was recorded on a separate phone, giving both outsider pairs a potential cross-device offset the insider pair does not have. Any difference found between their synchrony could therefore partly reflect this technical asymmetry rather than physiology alone — though the coarse time resolution most synchrony methods here use (60-second RMSSD windows) limits how much a likely sub-few-second offset could distort the result; it would matter more for anything comparing raw, sample-level timing, such as the ECG overlay.

This is a single triad, a single session — the result is best read as a pilot observation, not a confirmed group-level finding about students and lecturers.
